0:00–0:45Intro

Why we built this

"Thanks for making the time. What you're looking at is SeatBook — the desk-booking system replacing the spreadsheet-and-Slack-thread approach to hybrid seating. This walkthrough is short by design: about ten minutes, in two halves. First I'll show the app the way most of our people actually use it — booking a desk as an employee. Then I'll switch hats and show the admin console, where facilities and IT configure floors, seats, and access. Let's start on the employee side."

Dashboard

"This is home base for every employee. At a glance: whether they're booked in today, a week strip they can tap through, and a running stat on days in office versus target. Below that, their upcoming bookings with one-tap cancel or modify. This card — teammates in office today — is the one people ask for constantly: before you commute in, you can already see who else will actually be there. 1:30–2:45Employee

Book a Seat

"Let's book one from scratch — it's a three-step flow. Step one, pick dates, single day or a range. Step two, pick the office and floor. Step three is the part people actually like: an interactive floor map drawn from the real floor plan, where every bookable seat sits exactly where it sits in the office. Filter by amenity — dual monitor, standing desk, near a window — and the map updates live. Click a seat, confirm, done. Because the map matches the physical floor exactly, there's zero ambiguity about which literal desk you're sitting at Monday morning."

4:15–5:30Admin

Setting up the space

"Everything an employee just saw starts here. Admins define the physical hierarchy first — offices, buildings, floors — then upload the actual floor-plan file for each floor. The moment a layout is uploaded, the system reads the seat labels in that file and automatically sorts them: a desk, a private cabin, a conference room, based on naming convention. Nobody has to manually tag hundreds of seats one at a time."

Show on screen

  • Offices / Buildings / Floors tree

5:30–7:00Admin

Manage Spaces

"This is the heart of the admin experience. Every seat, cabin, and conference room on a floor lands here, split into tabs with live stat cards showing how many are configured versus how many still need attention. An admin can open a single seat and edit its type, bookable status, capacity, or amenities — or, more realistically, select fifty seats at once and bulk-edit them together: mark a row as hot-desks, tag a wing with a monitor amenity, take a bank of seats offline for renovation. Nothing goes live immediately, though. Changes are staged, and publishing a layout requires an effective date at least fifteen days out — on purpose, so facilities and comms have time to notify the floor before the seating map underneath them actually changes."

If it comes up

Backup talking points — not part of the timed script, use only if a question opens the door.

Guest visits

The visitor path isn't a bolt-on — it's a full guest-visit subsystem with its own email lifecycle for created, modified, and cancelled visits, sent to the guest, the host, and whoever booked it.

Auto-categorization

Seats are sorted into desks, cabins, and conference rooms by reading naming patterns in the uploaded floor plan itself — no manual tagging pass needed after an upload.

Permission model

Access isn't hardcoded per role — each user carries a granular permission list. That's what lets us introduce new roles later (front office, facilities) without rebuilding the access logic.

What's scaffolded

Front-office / visitor check-in and facilities-specific views already exist in the navigation structure but are placeholders today — a preview of roadmap direction, not a promise of a date.
